CREDIT CARDS

Count the purchase once. Keep cash for the card.

A credit-card purchase is spending. Paying the card later repays that debt; it is not a second purchase. A useful budget tracks both the spending and the cash needed for repayment.

By SpentOn · Updated 14 September 2026 · 3 minute read

What happens when you buy something on a card?

The purchase increases what you owe on the card and reduces the money left in its spending category. It does not immediately reduce your bank balance.

When a category has enough cash set aside, SpentOn keeps cash for the card payment as the purchase is recorded. That cash is still in your bank, but it is needed to repay the card.

FICTIONAL EXAMPLE · US DOLLARS

A $40 card purchase

You have already set aside $120 for Dining, then pay $40 for dinner with a credit card.

After recording the funded purchase
RecordAmount
Dining spending$40
Left in Dining$80
New card debt$40
Cash kept for this card purchase$40

Your bank balance has not changed yet. The $40 kept for the card is not additional money to spend.

Record the purchase and payment separately

When you pay that $40 from your bank to the card, your bank balance falls by $40 and the card debt falls by $40. Dining spending stays at $40.

Use Record card payment for that movement. Entering it as another Dining purchase would describe a second expense that did not happen.

SpentOn records the payment in your budget; it does not send it to the card issuer. Make and confirm your payment through your bank or issuer, then record the amount and date.

What if the purchase was not fully funded?

A card purchase can create debt without enough cash set aside to repay it. Your budget should show that gap instead of assuming the debt is covered.

Review the purchase and decide whether to move category money or set aside more cash when you have it. A salary scheduled for a future date is not available cash today.

A card refund also needs its own record. It reduces the related spending and card debt, or may create a credit on the card. It does not automatically add money to your bank account.

Statement due and total card debt are different

A statement covers a period set by the issuer. Purchases made after that period can add to the total card debt without belonging to the current statement due.

Enter statement information from the issuer and keep it separate from the total amount you owe and the cash you have set aside. SpentOn does not connect to your issuer or infer a payment due date from a billing-cycle day.

If you pay a shared bill with your card, you remain responsible for the full card debt. Friends’ unpaid shares are still amounts owed to you, not cash already available for the card payment. See the shared-expense example.

A few common questions

Does paying my credit card increase my spending total?

No. The recorded purchases determine spending. A correctly recorded card payment moves money from your bank to repay card debt without adding another expense.

Does cash set aside for a card payment leave my bank?

No. It remains in your bank until you make the payment. Setting it aside means that cash is already needed for the card and should not also be used to fund other spending.

Does SpentOn automatically pay my card?

No. You pay through your usual bank or card issuer and record the payment in SpentOn. The app cannot verify that an external payment was completed.
